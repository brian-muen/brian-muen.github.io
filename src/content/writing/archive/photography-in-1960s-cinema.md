---
title: "Photography in 1960s Cinema: An Analysis of Blowup and La Jetée"
date: 2024-12-01T12:00:00Z
description: "How Blowup and La Jetée use photography to question reality, memory, authorship, and the boundaries of cinema."
---

In the image-ridden age of modernity, we have begun to forget the element of the fantastical in the act of photography. With a light tap of the finger upon a digital interface, we can record the world around us at a moment's notice, one moment at a time. The mechanical click of the shutter that is emitted from our cell phones is a vestigial sound effect placing digital photography in a lineage that traces all the way back to 1839. That was the year when the freshly-invented daguerreotype was presented in the French Chamber of Deputies, inducing a rupture in the history and perceptions of representational systems (Benjamin, 1931/1999). It was now possible to mechanically preserve a slice of time in realistic detail, satisfying the obsession for realism previously sought through paintings (Bazin & Gray, 1960).

In his book *Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man*, Marshall McLuhan famously wrote that the "medium is the message" (McLuhan, 1964). With this statement, he hinted at the vital way in which media and content interact to produce meaning. The medium is not an empty container suited for communication, but a container with its own expectations and eccentricities that shapes what is being communicated. Thus, the ontology of a photograph becomes a question of importance. Is the photograph an authentic representation of reality, or is it a form that through the process of capture results in a new fictional reality? Throughout the history of photography, this is an issue that has repeatedly been addressed but never definitely answered.

Even in his 1931 essay "Little History of Photography," Walter Benjamin realized that inability to "read" the photograph would become the "illiteracy of the future" (Benjamin, 1931/1999, p. 527). The post-war period can be seen as a transitional period during which the methods of critique became increasingly suited to deal with the implications of mass reproduction and immateriality characteristic of the modern digital photograph (Nardelli, 2020).

The 1960s in Europe was a decade which Pierre Taminiaux characterized as "arguably the most turbulent period of post-World War II European history" (Taminiaux, 2011, p. 119). Thus, the question emerges as to how different films from this particular period represent the medium of photography through the related medium of film. In the midst of this flux, defined by Susan Sontag as a "new sensibility" in culture, *Blowup* by Michelangelo Antonioni and *La Jetée* by Chris Marker emerged during the 1960s as two films that placed a treatment of the photograph at its ideological center stage (Sontag, 1978).

It is crucial to engage with these films as a reflection of a specific temporal slice of cultural history. It was during the 1960s that society became eminently "postmodern" (Azuma, 2009). The decade was also in the beginnings of what Neil Postman defined as a new televisual age that replaced the previous typographic one (Postman, 2005). No longer was the written word the crucial form of communication; rather, images took center stage in an age where televisual media occupied a central function in cultural discourse. In such a cultural milieu, it became increasingly vital to provide a serious reflection on the ontology of photographs as a form of image.

*Blowup* and *La Jetée* are two such reflections. Through a close viewing, it becomes apparent that both movies reveal the fragmented view of reality provided by photographs and their tendency to blend the real and the imaginary. This conclusion will be teased out through a methodical inspection of the two movies and the context in which they were produced. First, we will analyze the depictions of photography as an act and the photograph as a medium in the two films. This will be followed by a discussion of how the depictions of photography interact with the filmic medium itself as a form of *mise en abyme*. Finally, we will survey the European cultural context of the 1960s and the rise of postmodernism that was experienced during that time.

As the meaning of a photograph for a viewer is possible only through the action of the photographer, it seems most suitable for us to begin our investigation with *Blowup*, which is a narrative about the photographer himself.

*Blowup* (1966) directed by Antonioni follows an unnamed fashion photographer (identified as Thomas in the credits) who happens to snap seemingly innocent photographs of two lovers in a park. Returning to his studio, a detail in the photographic print compels him to produce successively scaled blow-ups of the scene that guides him to construct a narrative of murder. Yet, by the end of the film, nothing seems to be resolved, as the body is never found and a single blow up constitutes all evidence of the act. In this way, *Blowup* casts a lingering doubt on the authenticity of the photograph as an object of interpretation, while also revealing the hidden reality that can lie behind such a recording of the past.

The murder mystery component of the film unravels as the photographer notices a detail in the photo still he develops, hinting at a hidden violence within a seemingly peaceful scene. There is some irony in this discovery, as Thomas at one point proposes to use the photos of the lovers as a blissful ending to a photobook replete with images of poverty and despair. In fact, Thomas at one point admits to not knowing what happened because "I didn't see" (Antonioni, 1966, 1:29:45).

This notion of a hidden reality not revealed to the human gaze is delineated by Benjamin in "Little History" as the optical unconscious (Benjamin, 1931/1999). In this construction, the camera is able to unveil a new layer of reality that was previously inaccessible. As such, photography becomes an extension of human vision, subverting its perceptual and temporal limits. In fact, Benjamin further likens the photograph to a crime scene, which closely resembles Antonioni's depiction of the medium within the film. Inspecting the photographic crime scenes with a magnifying glass and ruminating over a glass of liquor, Antonioni's portrayal of Thomas calls to mind the conventional image of the detective in cinema.

It is notable that the critical blow up which Thomas interprets to be the dead man's body resembles the frenetic abstract portraits painted by his friend Bill. In an early scene, Bill notes to Thomas that finding meaning in his paintings is akin to "finding a clue in a detective story" (Antonioni, 1966, 17:23). Indeed, after his studio is ransacked, this singular ambiguous blow up is all that is left to evidence the act of murder. Thus, Thomas is figured as a detective in the photographic age, where the photograph itself has become the scene of crime. Enhanced by an optical unconscious, it is in the monochromatic confusion of low resolution blow ups that Thomas finds the clues to piece together his narrative of murder.

Fittingly, the interpretation of photographs is a theme that Antonioni highlights throughout the depiction of photography in *Blowup*. As previously noted, we see a juxtaposition of the remaining photographic evidence with the abstraction of Bill's works that for him "don't mean anything when I do them," assuming meaning only in retrospective viewing (Antonioni, 1966, 17:00). Thomas's interaction with the photograph, then, is suggested to be riddled with subjective interpretation. It is only through the deconstruction of the original roll film into carefully produced blow ups that the narrative of murder is able to emerge.

Rather than objectively observing reality through a photograph, Thomas actively constructs a narrative of what has taken place. By the end of the film, not even the physical corpse remains as evidence of the murder; rather, the hazy photographic representation of the corpse singularly remains. It is the interpretation of the photograph that allows any possibility of narrative to take shape. Thus, the photograph is depicted as a subject of interpretation far removed from any notion of mechanical objectivity.

It follows that the relation between the photograph and its referent is an issue that is also brought out within the film. The idea of mimesis is immediately hinted at in the opening scene, where the shot follows a group of mimes before transitioning to the introduction of Thomas, who has disguised himself as a member of the working class in a desire to capture candid photos of their struggles. This juxtaposition immediately establishes the sense of mimesis that Thomas engages in through his occupation as a photographer. Just as the mimes mimic human actions, he attempts to mimic a social class in order to provide a seemingly more authentic depiction of a societal reality.

This mimetic aspect loops back upon itself as the act of photography itself is imbued with the notion of mimesis. The photograph purports to mimic a time-slice in the present reality and preserve it for some unspecified future. The objectivity of such mimetic forms is a theme explored within the film. At his studio, Thomas stages fashion shoots of models in a room full of glass partitions, suggesting the space as highly artificial. Notably, it is the scene when he captures the moment of murder in the park that Thomas is seen as relenting all control of the staging of the subject. He is seen weaving in and out of the trees, integrating himself into the natural world that contrasts with the unnaturalness of his studio.

Thus, we see that Antonioni constructs a difference between the photographer's more didactic and uncontrolled recordings of the reality around him. However, although the didactic is intuitively seen as less objective due to its enforcing of a specific authorial notion, Antonioni illuminates the fact that more "natural" recordings of reality are just as susceptible to the subjectivity of a human agent. There is simply a shift of the interpretive act from the photographer to the viewer.

This tenuous and uncertain mimesis of reality afforded by photography is confirmed in the final scene where the mimes from the opening play a game of tennis with an unseen ball. The unseen ball lands in the grass beside Thomas, and he joins the mimicry by tossing it back. Thus, his final act in the film is also one of mimesis. In the final shot of Thomas staring into the distance, the diegetic sound of a bouncing of a tennis ball is heard in the background. This hallucinatory use of sound suggests that, in the final analysis, it is Thomas who informs reality through his involvement in the reality he perceives around him, both as photographer and viewer. Perceived reality becomes inseparable from the subjectivity of the human agent. Thus, the shattering of a single objective and universal reality is seemingly evoked by Antonioni through symbolism he utilizes within the frames of *Blowup*.

Meanwhile, *La Jetée* (1962) directed by Chris Marker incorporates its treatment of photography not through a photographer, but through the material of the photograph itself. Except for a brief moment of motion, the film is composed entirely of still photos that intermittently transition from one to another. One can imagine it as a film with fewer frames per second and lessened continuity between those frames. The title card itself terms the film as *un photo-roman*, emphasizing its existence as a narrative constituted from only still photos.

The protagonist from a post-apocalyptic future becomes an experimental subject for time travel due to an indelible memory from his childhood where he witnessed the death of an unknown individual. However, by the end of the film, it becomes apparent that the memory was the scene of the man's own death cast back from the future. Thus, *La Jetée* thematically links photography to the nature of memory, while also emphasizing the unknowns beyond the frame of both.

In contrast to the optical unconscious established through *Blowup*, Chris Marker utilizes the structure of *La Jetée* to emphasize the limits of the photographic medium. As the protagonist does not understand the scene of his own death until the moment it happens, the photograph is seen as a partial representation of a past reality that is confined by its frames. In effect, the off-screen of the photograph is seen as an inaccessible extension of the reality in the photograph. It thus aligns with the notion of the off-screen as an inaccessible interpretive space that is "open to the roaming presence of ghosts" (Beugnet, 2022).

In the film, this ghost is the spectre of the protagonist's own fated death as his future self. It is only the omniscient narrator, who has access to multiple fragments of history through the projected photographs, who can explain the off-screen to the audience. As this is contrasted to the individual who dwells in reality, possessing no such omniscience, it is clear that Marker points to a limitation of the photograph as being unable to capture reality in its entirety, thereby fragmenting it.

Furthermore, Marker explicitly links the photograph to the phenomenon of memory. Marker himself once stated the following: "I remember the images I filmed. They have substituted themselves for my memory" (O'Riley, 1998, p. 77). The photograph is seen as a prosthetic memory that extends the biological memory of the brain. As such, the still photos from the opening that apparently derive from the protagonist's memory immediately situate the photograph as analogous in nature.

Indeed, just as memory is known to be reconstructive in nature, assembling itself from traces, photography also fragments the past due to its existence as snapshots of individual moments. It is up to the omniscient narrator to piece together fragments into a narrative, filling in what has been lost in time. The previously discussed loss of information in the off-screen that is observed within photography is thus analogous to the loss of information through forgetting.

However, beyond its symbolic relation to memory, photography also directly interacts with the formation of biological memory. In the opening scene, as the photographic memories of the protagonist are displayed, the narrator notes that "nothing sorts out memories from ordinary moments" (Marker, 1962, 2:15). However, it becomes increasingly clear in the film that this is no longer the case in the age of photography. The act of photography itself functions to separate memory from ordinary moments. The photographer decides in advance which moments are worthy of recording, thus resulting in the archive of photographic memory from which the narrator is able to string together the film's narrative. In this way, Marker is able to draw out the complex interplay between photography and memory in contemporary culture.

It is crucial that photography also serves as a specific type of memory: that of *memento mori* (O'Riley). In fact, Barthes suggested that death is a condition perpetually hidden in photographs (Barthes, 1980/1981). As death implies a has-been, the photograph is always seen through the lens of the past tense. The revelation that the photographs from the protagonist's memory are the images of his own death reflects this sentiment.

Furthermore, there is a scene in the film where the protagonist travels to the past to visit a museum filled with preserved animals with a woman from his memories. In the stills that portray this scene, the juxtaposition between the preserved animals and the two characters seems to suggest that they are being similarly preserved in the museum of photographic memory. The act of photography, due to its expectation of preservation, immediately situates the subject as an artifact of the past. As such, we see that the way in which the photograph interacts with time is lucidly brought out throughout *La Jetée*.

It is apparent that the depiction of photography within the two films becomes a form of *mise en abyme* that reflexively provides a commentary on the medium of film itself. However, whereas film is seemingly steeped in the past tense, it can be said that cinema reanimates the past as a present reality through an approximate "illusion of motion" (O'Riley, 1998; Gaycken, 2011, p. 361). However, as film can be deconstructively seen as simply still photography displayed at twenty four frames per second, it is clear that there is a point where one medium blends into the other.

Antonioni uses the titular blow ups to provide an insight to the relation between the photograph and film. At one point of the film, the blow ups fully occupy the screen, and one transitions to another in a montage that substitutes the cinematographic image. The interpreted narrative of the murder is therefore played to the audience through the ordering of the photographic sequence of blow ups. There is thus the existence of a miniature *photo-roman* within the film itself. However, despite the subtle merging of the two mediums, Antonioni contrasts the monochromatic blow ups with the use of colour film in the rest of *Blowup*, reinforcing the notion of film as much more vivid and seemingly steeped in the present tense.

Marker's engagement with the ontology of film through depiction of photography is much more implicit. The blending of photography and film is seen from the beginning. In the initial shots of the movie, it is not clearly established that the images presented are merely photo stills. Marker intentionally chooses settings that require no visible movement to evidence the passage of time to create this sense of ambiguity. This liminality between the two mediums is eventually built up to a climactic scene where the woman who haunts the protagonist's memory is seen in movement for a brief moment as she gazes into the eye of the camera. Thus, this tender moment suggests that the reanimation of the past seen in film is inherently linked to a subjective and the emotional engagement in the viewer.

It is clear that both films are situated in the interstices of photography and film. Rather than clearly distinct mediums, these films suggest that they can instead be seen as representational methods originating along a shared spectrum. The singular difference is temporal, with film being traditionally displayed at twenty-four frames per second. Moreover, this temporal distinction is further developed due to the seemingly different tenses with which the spectator tends to orient the two mediums. Crucially, however, it becomes apparent that the treatment of the ontology of the photograph provided by both films can be reflexively applied back onto the medium of film itself as a form of representation.

As products of a decade that saw the rise of postmodernity as a cultural condition, it is interesting to note the undertones of the postmodern contained within these two works. Specifically, Japanese theorist Azuma identifies the two identifying characteristics of postmodern culture as the prevalence of simulacra and the acceptance of a grand nonnarrative (Azuma, 2009). Initially conceptualized by Jean Baudrillard, simulacra are cultural artifacts that are neither original or copy (Baudrillard, 1983). Meanwhile, the grand nonnarrative is defined in contrast to the modernist notion of a grand narrative that imbues meaning in everything; instead, meaning is said to be found in smaller narratives that do not cohere into a totalizing grand narrative. Both postmodern elements can be teased out from *Blowup* and *La Jetée*.

Benjamin expressed concern over the consequences of reproduction in contemporary capitalist society, which he saw as devaluing the aura of an original which was derived from its authenticity as a specific cultural and temporal product (Benjamin, 1931/1999). Specifically, the notion of simulacra is materialized in the form of the blow ups that Thomas uses to construct his narrative of murder. The blow ups can be seen as derivatives of the original, but neither wholly original or a carbon copy.

Meanwhile, in *La Jetée*, this notion of the simulacra is embodied in the still photographs themselves. Depicted as moments from the protagonist's memory, the photographs have no established origin within the reality of the film but rather exist as a given. Crucially, the reality reconstructed from memory is itself neither wholly original or a copy. The relationship between photography and memory within the film is therefore symbolic. As the photographic stills apparently represent this uncertain reality imbued within memory, these images with which the narrator constructs the story are themselves a type of simulacra.

Furthermore, these films seem to fit within the postmodernist database model of consumption proposed by Azuma, which proposes the rise of the grand nonnarrative. In this case, the photograph is the cultural object being consumed. In Azuma's construction, with the shattering of the grand narrative in postmodern society, meaning in small narratives can be derived only from the combination of database elements as it suits the individual (Azuma, 2009).

Similarly, in both films, the photographs become the elements from which narratives within both films are constructed. The photograph can be seen as an object that is recontextualized in a way that suits the smaller narrative that the individual seeks to consume. In *Blowup*, this is the photographer's narrative of murder while in *La Jetée*, it is the narrator's story of science fictional time-travel.

Notably, in *La Jetée*, the narration is vital to conveying a sense of a storyline. It thus makes sense in this context that Marker was known to stray away from the movement of *cinéma vérité* of the time that sought to bring a more objective approach to film by eschewing traditional narration and providing only raw footage (Grimshaw & Ravetz, 2009). Perhaps in a prescient move, Marker predicted the postmodern societal turn that was to come, instead declaring "ciné, ma vérité," translated as "cinema, my truth" (Medeiros et al., 2015, p. 9). Through this statement, the subjectivity of visualization brought out with an emphasis on the notion of authorship. Thus, this aligns with the postmodernist notion that one is able to assume truth only in a specific individual context.

As has been established throughout this essay, it is clear that both *Blowup* and *La Jetée* were specific cultural products that used depictions of photography to both reflect and reflect upon the postmodern culture that was developing at that period of time in Europe. The essential questions of the ontology of photography and film are becoming ever more critical in our contemporary society. As Benjamin noted, "the camera is getting smaller and smaller," thus democratizing the status of the photographer in photography (Benjamin, 1931/1999, p. 527). As anyone can become the photographer, it becomes important to acknowledge what it means to assume such a position.

Moreover, in modern society, images as representations of reality have become ever more proliferated. In fact, the image has evolved from the photograph to film and now to the generative "images" of artificial intelligence. What should we then regard as photographic and how is this linked to our notions of truth in modern society?

In the final analysis, both films seem to represent photographs as corpses of a past reality, both highly subjective and fragmented. Whatever truth or meaning can be gained from these is suggested to be up to the spectator, which has become the status of every single member of modern society.
